The Quiet Cost of Notifications

By Dana Reyes

Every interruption carries a price that is rarely written down. A notification arrives, attention breaks, and the work that was underway has to be rebuilt from memory. The rebuilding is the expensive part, and it is almost never counted.

Researchers who study task switching describe a recovery period that follows each interruption. The length of that period depends on how deep the original task was. Shallow work recovers quickly. Deep work does not recover quickly at all, and sometimes it does not recover on the same day.

What the numbers miss

Most measurements of productivity count output, because output is easy to see. They rarely count the cost of reassembly, because reassembly looks like nothing at all from the outside. A person staring at a screen and reconstructing a mental model appears identical to a person doing nothing.

This is why teams that measure only throughput tend to under-invest in uninterrupted time. The loss is real, but it never appears in the column where losses are tallied, so it goes unmanaged year after year.

A more honest accounting

A better approach starts by treating attention as the scarce resource rather than hours. Hours are abundant and interchangeable. Attention is neither. When attention is treated as the constraint, the batching of interruptions stops looking like a courtesy and starts looking like basic arithmetic.

The cheapest interruption is the one that was never sent.

Batching is arithmetic, not etiquette

Grouping interruptions into a handful of predictable moments does not make a team less responsive. It makes the cost of responsiveness legible, which is the first step to deciding how much of it is actually wanted.

Where to start on Monday

Pick one recurring interruption and give it a scheduled home. Measure nothing for a fortnight. Then ask the people doing the deepest work whether the week felt different, because they are the instrument that actually detects the change.

None of this requires new tools. It requires only that the cost be written down somewhere it can be seen, and then defended like any other budget that matters to the people who keep it.
