Measuring What Models Actually Do

By Dana Reyes

Benchmarks are easy to quote and hard to interpret. A single number compresses a great deal of judgement about what was measured, on which distribution, and against whose baseline. The charts below are the part of this piece that carries the argument, so they are worth reading before the prose that surrounds them.

The first thing to notice is that the gap narrows considerably once the evaluation is held fixed. That is not a claim about capability so much as a claim about measurement, and it is the reason the second chart matters more than the first.

Held-out sets drift. That drift is usually slow enough to ignore over a single release and fast enough to matter across three, which is why the same benchmark can look flat and then suddenly move without anything about the underlying method changing at all.

The third chart is the one most often misread. It shows a ratio, not a level, and ratios have a habit of looking dramatic exactly where the denominator is smallest. Read the axis before reading the slope.

None of this argues that benchmarks are useless. It argues that a benchmark is a measuring instrument with a calibration story, and that the calibration story is usually the interesting part of the result rather than an appendix to it.

What we would measure instead

A better report would state the distribution, the baseline, the number of trials, and the variance across them, and it would publish the failures alongside the successes so that a reader can judge where the instrument stops being trustworthy.
